# The Robot Brain Might Already Exist. It's A Coding Agent.

The next breakthrough in robotics could arrive as an update to your coding agent. At Stanford, researchers connected a general coding agent to a robot through a visual interface without any additional robot specific training. And I can't underline that enough. No extra training. It completed 24 of 25 trials across five physical tasks. OpenAI has now reorganized its robotic research around a related bet. Better general AI can help produce better robots. And I think the way to understand what's happening is an old idea called the bitter lesson. Richard Sutton wrote the bitter lesson in 2019. His argument was that AI researchers keep running into the exact same problem over and over again. We figure out what makes a task hard. We build our understanding into a system and we get something that works. But then a more general approach comes along that can keep improving with more computation, more learning, and more search. Eventually, it overtakes the thing we spent years carefully designing. That's the bitter part. You put all this expertise into solving a problem, and an approach that can just keep learning just walks right past you over time. Sutton traces this through chess, through Go, through speech recognition, and ultimately through computer vision. The lesson is to pay attention to what keeps getting better as you scale it. And in robotics, that creates a really interesting possibility. We may be building some of a robot's intelligence while we're making AI better at everything else. Better visual understanding, better tool use, better ability to work through a lengthy task and notice when something went wrong. Those abilities matter when an agent is using a computer, but they can also matter when it's using a robot arm. Now, that doesn't mean every single specialized robotics model is ultimately doomed. A model trained on huge amounts of physical experience can follow the bitter lesson, too. The question is, how much do you need to build specifically for a robot when a general purpose AI model might be able to do much of the same work? That's exactly the bad behind OpenAI's recent reorganization of their own robotics team. The company confirmed that its robotics researchers were moving into main research with applied robotics and hardware moving into its applied organization. It says robotics is still absolutely part of the plan. It explanation is that general frontier models are becoming more useful for robotics work. Cue the bitter lesson theme song guys. There may be several reasons for the decision and the reporting of course does discuss some of the financial pressures involved. But you can see the technical logic. If the people improving your general model are also improving that model's ability to operate a robot essentially for free, the robotics effort can greatly benefit from just embedding into the work happening across the company. You don't need every useful advance to come from the robotics team if it's benefiting robotics just to build your general model. Think about the amount of effort already going into making agents better at using software. They have to interpret what they're looking at, choose an action, remember what they've tried, and recognize when they're stuck. If some of that work carries over to a robot, you get a much much larger group of people contributing to that progress, even when they aren't working on robotics. That's a really powerful reason to want this particular approach to work. And Stanford's VIA project gives us a way to see how this actually works in practice. So VIA stands for visual interface agent. In the physical experiment, the researchers used codeex Astro 6 and a commercial stretch 4 robot. They gave the agent a visual interface through which it could look at the scene and choose where that robot gripper ought to go in time and space. Picture a drawer handle, right? This is an illustration of the process, right? You want the robot to sort of reach over and grab the drawer handle and pull it out. The agent sees the camera view and places a virtual gripper where it thinks the real gripper needs to end up. Maybe it's a touch high. Maybe from another view it's a touch too far forward. The agent is able to then adjust that target before the robot actually moves in time and space. Effectively, the agent is planning. Once the agent is satisfied, it then tells the controller to move the real gripper to the chosen destination. Then it checks again. Did the gripper actually catch the handle? Did it miss the handle? Does it need to adjust before pulling? You get the idea. The next decision comes after you see what happened rather than just continuing with a set sequence. Now, the model isn't figuring out every single motor movement moment by movement moment. Instead, the controller handles moving the arm toward the target, and just choosing the target is a meaningful part of the job. So, you can move an arm perfectly and still accomplish nothing if you're aimed at the wrong place. Right? Then you just make it tilt. The agent has to connect what it sees with what it wants to happen, pick an action, and check the result. And the robot arm is taking care of the lot of the mechanics. In a way, it's similar to our brains. I don't think about which muscles to move when I move my arm. I just pick the target, and it goes there. This should sound familiar if you've watched an agent use a computer. It looks at what's available. It uses a tool. It checks what change, and it decides what to do next. In this case, all that's happening is the tool is moving a physical machine. And the consequences, of course, they're physical, too. Seeing a virtual gripper in the right place doesn't prove you're going to get a good grip on the drawer. You actually have to make the move and find out if you can move the drawer. And so, that's what the researchers did. In VIA's physical experiment, a coding agent succeeded in 24 of 25 trials across five tasks without any extra training on robotics. Now, sure, that's a small study, but I've seen a number of them like that that basically point to the idea that an existing generalpurpose agent can perform these physical tasks without another round of robot specific fine-tuning. And we're seeing this replicated in different ways all across the robotics industry right now. And this is where the bitter lesson enters, right? The agent had already gone through a huge amount of general training. I'm sure it had encountered some robotics information in there. That's just part of the better lesson. What the researchers showed is that they could take generalpurpose capabilities the model had developed for other uses like coding, give it a way to operate the machine and get extremely useful behavior first crack out of the box. They did not have to start over and teach it everything about the task at all. There's a nice example of one of the tasks in the paper the researchers wrote up. In a task where the robot wiped a mark off of a table, its own gripper blocked the view. So, the agent moved the gripper away to check whether the table was clean. And in one attempt, holding the wipe wasn't working well enough. So, it left the wipe on the table and pushed it with a closed gripper. That's the kind of sophisticated learning behavior you want to see. Try something, see whether it worked, and adjust what you're doing. And that's what a general purpose agent was able to do with robots without any specific training. And that last bit matters a ton for the direction of AI research in 2027. If you're improving the model's ability to understand a picture and follow a complicated instruction or recover from a bad tool call, how much of that improvement becomes useful in a lot of other directions, including robotics? We now have concrete ways to investigate that. And it means a model upgrade can change what our existing robotics and hardware stacks can do. Now, of course, you still have to connect the model. Well, a November study called just RUA kept the underlying model fixed and substantially improved its simulation score by simply improving the software around the model. And so, I don't want to sit here and say you can just plug your agent into anything and it will magically work. But if you have a good surprise harness around your agent that's tuned to robotics and you can let the agent focus on learning and taking action, you're in a pretty good spot with today's frontier models. even if you just pop them into robotics for the first time. So there are two things happening together here. First, general models are getting more capable. And second, researchers are finding better ways to make those capabilities useful on robotics. But there's one big practical problem that we still need to get through from a robotics perspective, and that's speed. Now, this is not the same setup as the experimental setup with the VIA that I described, but it illustrates why getting the right answer, getting correctness is not the whole way to solve the problem. We need speed to interact with the real world. If you're moving an object on a quiet workbench, you may think that you have time to stop and to understand the problem and then move the object. But if the object starts sliding off the edge, if you're in a real manufacturing situation, you don't have the time. The controller must be able to keep the arm moving and move it correctly in real time to respond to emerging situations. You still need a system that can respond quick enough for the job that you've given it, especially around edge cases. And ultimately, speed affects whether the job is worth doing at all. A robot that eventually finishes while someone stands there supervising it may consume more attention than it saves. You need to know how long the whole attempt took, how often someone intervened, and what happened when it made a mistake. So for people building in robotics, this is the year of the bitter lesson. But it doesn't mean that everything is immediately solved. You have to be able to figure out how to take these generalpurpose agents, hook them up through good harnesses to robotics tools and then figure out how to run them at a high enough token speed that they can respond in real time to the situation your robot is likely to encounter and to ensure that they're able to drive the solution to the kinds of physical real world problems your robot is supposed to solve. Now, we have all seen the pictures of the dancing robots. We know that robots can be very fast, but just coordinating physical movement is not the same thing as being able to solve complicated problems. And that's why ultimately we haven't seen household robots take off yet. So for everyone following AI, this is a reason to look beyond the most obvious use of a new model.
