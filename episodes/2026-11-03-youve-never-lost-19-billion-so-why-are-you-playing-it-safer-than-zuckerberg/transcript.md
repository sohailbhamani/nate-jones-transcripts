# You've Never Lost $19 Billion. So Why Are You Playing It Safer Than Zuckerberg?

Mark Zuckerberg renamed his whole company for the metaverse, lost tens of billions of dollars, over $19 million in one year in 2025, by the way. Watched the whole world mock it and call it a flop. And he didn't respond by getting careful. He swung again, and now 10 million people have set up Muse in 6 weeks. Look, I've never lost $19 billion. You probably haven't either. And yet, we're still playing it safer than he is. You can think whatever you want about Mark the person. What I want to talk about is Mark the founder, and why AI rewards the people who swing big. If you strip out the money, there are three things behind that success that you and I can copy. Who they hired, how obsessive they got about the details nobody asked for, and the big one, the lesson that Mark Zuckerberg paid tens of billions of dollars to show he learned. After you miss, swing again. It doesn't matter how smart the models get at this point. Agency matters, and the story of Muse is the story of agency. When I covered Muse in September, I talked about the work it had done for me. I named mine Marvin after Douglas Adams, and it helped me cancel $1,285 in annual subscription spending, which, hey, thanks, Marvin. That was a real job that I had been putting off. I also talked about how approachable the product felt. Now we have a ton more people using it, and a much fuller account of how the team built Muse. And I think that account helps explain why this moment, this attempt, is reaching people in a way the metaverse never did. To understand the size of the change here, please think back to what Zuckerberg has been trying to do for a long time. Meta reaches enormous numbers of people, billions, but its apps mostly live on devices and operating systems other companies control and manage. You can build one of the biggest businesses in the world, and you still can have Apple change something that affects how you reach your customers or make your money. Mark Zuckerberg wrote about that specific frustration explicitly in his 2024 letter on open source. He wants more control over the next generation of computing. And that ambition goes back at least to the Oculus acquisition in 2014, if not earlier. Back then, when Facebook bought Oculus as a virtual reality company, Mark was talking about the platforms that would follow mobile. Mobile was early then. And by 2021, he had renamed the company around the metaverse. Imagine how much conviction that takes. Your company's name, its investment priorities, billions and billions of dollars, a huge part of your public identity, all attached to a future you believe people are going to want. And all we remember is the meme of the digital Mark Zuckerberg that's heavily pixelated by, I think it was the Eiffel Tower. And most people don't ever follow you to the metaverse. They don't reorganize their social lives around virtual worlds. They don't decide that this is how they want to spend their evenings. There are people who love virtual reality, sure, and there are some useful things we're going to get out of it, but the mass market platform Mark described just didn't arrive. Reality Labs, the division responsible for headsets, for glasses, for related research, lost 19.2 billion dollars in 2025 alone. And by the way, that is just one year's worth of losses. It gives you a sense of the cost of this search that Mark has been on, but it's not the whole bill. It's easy to say at this point that Meta should learn from its failures, that Mark made a big failure, and Mark should change directions and get more cautious and focus on Meta's core cash flow. At this point, changing direction, when you've invested this much, when you've named your company, it can feel like an admission that everybody was right. And you end up finding yourself defending the original vision long after you should have changed it. Or maybe you just try and hope everybody forgets about it, and you abandon it, and you leave it up. Mark Zuckerberg was not cautious. Mark Zuckerberg did not abandon his approach. Instead, Mark Zuckerberg took an already successful advertising business where he could have focused his attention, made money, and spent decades avoiding any other promise at scale, and been wildly successful by any measure. No, he kept pursuing a platform. Meta's AI and hardware bets have overlapped, and the work on glasses is going to continue, but fundamentally, Muse is the bet that Mark is making that we will all enter the world he wants to build. Mark came back. Mark took another swing.
