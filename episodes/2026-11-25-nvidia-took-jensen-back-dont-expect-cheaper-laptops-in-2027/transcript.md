Jensen Hong resigned from Nvidia on November 12th. 11 days later, the board voted unanimously to bring him back. Nvidia says it received input from senior US officials before making that decision. Think about that for a second. The US government weighed in on who should run a chip company. I think we just found out that Jensen Hang was too big to fail. If you want to understand why Washington cared who was running a chip company, look at what the AI buildout is doing to the rest of the economy. AI demand has grown so fast that the companies building data centers are now competing with everybody else for memory, for power, and for money. That's what connects Jensen's return to the price of your next laptop. It's what I'd pay attention to if you're deciding how you should budget for AI next year or where to place a career over the next two years. But let's start with your wallet because this is the part you can already feel every time you go to an electronic store. A year ago, memory made up about 20% of the parts cost of a budget smartphone. You know what it is now? Counterpoint says it's at 54% now. Global smartphone shipments have fallen 20% year-over-year. They are the lowest third quarter total since 2013. And Counterpoint also says the global average retail price of a phone is up 20% so far this year. Gartner forecast a 17% rise in PC prices for 2026. Probably more next year. So sub$100 phones are getting squeezed out. Laptop prices are getting squeezed. Why? Look at what happened to the memory industry in the third quarter. Counterpoint put it at nearly 200 billion. Up 369% from a year earlier. Wouldn't that have been a good business to be in? Counterpoint says higher [snorts] selling prices were the main reason revenue rose. The amount of memory shipped, did the price actually pull that up? No, because it's really sticky. It's hard to make chips. Memory grew only about 4% from the second quarter to the third. So, it increased, but not nearly as much as the price. The contract price for one common 16 gigabit DDR5 chip went from $8.70 to $48 in 11 months. A modern memory factory takes years to build and it can cost billions. When demand goes absolutely vertical and the factories are maxed, prices are rationing, but supply is not going up. Now, here's the technical wrinkle that explains why this has hit your phone and laptop prices so hard this year. Back when AI was mostly just the humans typing the short questions into chat boxes, which was not that long ago, most of the attention was on specialized high bandwidth memory sitting next to Nvidia GPUs. Very expensive, but a little bit separated out from all of the consumer gear we use day-to-day. But in 2026, more AI work has shifted to autonomous agents. Software running in loops for hours, browsing, calling tools, writing, and testing code overnight. the stuff I talk about and Agentic workloads. People don't know this. They don't just burn GPU. They also use generalurpose CPU servers that need conventional memory like DDR5 or LPDDR. Say that five times fast. That puts them in competition for some of the same capacity as smartphone and laptop makers. As cloud providers secured more and more and more of that supply, phone and PC makers had less room to buy. And now you're paying for that shortage at checkout because everyone's bidding each other up. And it's not just memory. That same collision is hitting electricity and it's hitting local communities. GE Venova's gas power equipment backlog and reservations have nearly doubled in a single year. And they're not alone. They went from 62 gawatt to 122 gawatt. Berkeley Lab found that new power projects are often taking more than 5 years to go from asking to connect to the grid to actually running. So an industry trade group estimates US construction needs about 350,000 additional workers this year and I bet more next year. And this is what it actually means to say AI broke parts of our real economy. AI started bidding for the same memory wafers, power turbines, grid capacity, and electricians that everybody else needs.
